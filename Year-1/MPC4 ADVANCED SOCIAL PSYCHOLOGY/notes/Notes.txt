**Attribution Theory in Education – Notes**

---

**1. Definition & Importance**
- Attribution theory (Weiner) explains how people interpret causes of success/failure.
- Strongly influences academic motivation and self-perception.

**2. Three Dimensions of Attribution**
- **Internal vs. External:** Cause originates within the person (e.g., effort, ability) or environment (e.g., luck, task difficulty).
- **Stable vs. Unstable:** Cause is consistent over time (e.g., ability) or can change (e.g., effort, luck).
- **Controllable vs. Uncontrollable:** Person can influence the cause (e.g., effort) or cannot (e.g., innate ability, luck).

**3. Key Factors**
- **Ability:** Internal, stable, uncontrollable.
- **Task Difficulty:** External, stable, uncontrollable.
- **Effort:** Internal, unstable, controllable.
- **Luck:** External, unstable, uncontrollable.

**4. Impact on Motivation**
- Students are most motivated when they attribute success to effort (internal, unstable, controllable) and failure to lack of effort.
- Attributing success only to ability can reduce motivation to try harder.
- Repeated failure despite effort can harm motivation and self-concept.

**5. Guidelines for Teachers**
- Encourage students to see effort as the path to success.
- Help students attribute failures to factors they can control (e.g., effort, strategy).
- Avoid making students believe lack of ability is the reason for failure.
- Define effort as effective learning, not just time spent.
- Avoid excessive competition; ensure effort leads to success.
- Evaluate students partly on effort, but ensure effort leads to real achievement.
- Foster an internal locus of control—students should believe their actions matter.

**6. Changing Negative Self-Attributions**
- Students convinced of low ability may dismiss successes as luck.
- Changing such beliefs requires time and support to alter self-concept.

**7. Practical Application**
- Teachers can use attribution retraining and reinforce effort-based attributions to boost motivation and productivity.

**Additional Concepts Related to Attribution Theory – Notes**

---

**1. Learning Goals vs. Performance Goals**
- **Learning goals:** Focus on increasing competence; encourage seeking challenges and increased effort after failure.
- **Performance goals:** Focus on gaining approval or avoiding disapproval; lead to challenge avoidance and negative reactions to failure (e.g., learned helplessness, self-handicapping).
- Encouraging learning goals is more beneficial for motivation and resilience.

**2. Learned Helplessness**
- Expectation that one’s actions cannot lead to success, often due to repeated failure.
- More common with performance goals and ability attributions.
- Leads to reduced motivation and effort.

**3. Self-Handicapping**
- Creating obstacles to success (e.g., not practicing, substance use) to protect self-esteem.
- If failure occurs, blame is placed on the obstacle; if success, it is seen as overcoming adversity.
- Reduces actual learning and performance.
- Can be influenced by peer culture or school environment.

**4. Expectancy-Valence Models**
- Motivation depends on the value of the goal (valence) and the perceived likelihood of success (expectancy).
- Highest motivation often occurs with moderate probability of success.
- Interacts with attribution theory: beliefs about causes of success/failure affect expectancy and effort.

**5. Practical Implications**
- Teachers should emphasize learning goals, effort attributions, and self-efficacy.
- Avoid overemphasizing performance goals and normative comparisons.
- Address cultural and peer influences that may encourage self-handicapping.
